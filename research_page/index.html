Satellite Supply Signals

Can free satellite data estimate changes in physical commodity supply before the market prices them in? Sixteen research tracks, from oil tanks and copper smelters to cocoa, wheat and soybean fields, plus hurricanes, each tested against what officials, companies and analysts already knew at the time.

Sentinel-1 · Sentinel-2 · MODIS · Landsat · UmbraRules fixed before outcomesHeld-back data for scoring
Short answer: not yet.

Free imagery reliably shows whether something is happening: a mine or smelter halting, a plant being built, machines working a pit. It does not measure how much is produced. Markets with weaker official data (cocoa, Russian wheat, Indian pulses) were no easier, and hurricane forecasts are priced on the day they appear. On held-back data only one signal beat an official forecast: field-level corn yields in Iowa and Illinois, by a small margin over four years. None beat the analysts' consensus or made money after costs.

What we learned

  1. Imagery answers "is it happening?", not "how much?" This held at 10 m and at 33 cm, for mines, construction and equipment.
  2. For metals, earthmoving is the wrong supply measure. Copper output follows ore grade and plant throughput; even Escondida's own reported tonnes mined correlate +0.01 with its copper output.
  3. Company events can't be front-run with free data. An early warning is worth about 12% per event for single-mine companies, but listed miners disclose within a day, and free radar archives arrive weeks to months later.
  4. Weak official data didn't make it easier. In cocoa, Russian wheat and Indian pulses, local analysts already fill the gap, or the shock had a cause the history didn't contain (cocoa's 2023 disease outbreak). Hurricane forecasts are priced the day they appear.
  5. Crops fit imagery better, but the benchmark is strong. US yield surprises against analysts move prices (about −0.9% per 1% surprise for corn), yet neither public crop ratings nor the satellite model predicted them out of sample. Brazilian revisions barely move CBOT at all.

What's left

  • Operating-status alerts for specific plants and mines. Free imagery flags shutdowns and restarts, sometimes before the press (Altonorte went dark 10 days before the first report). Useful for risk monitoring, not as a supply forecast.
  • Field-level corn. The one model that beat an official forecast, narrowly. It needs forward years and then a national version.
  • Large, slow-to-report physical shocks, like citrus after a direct hurricane hit, tracked as a forward test.
